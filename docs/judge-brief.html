5. Technical questions the panel will ask

Q1. Why EfficientNetB0 and not ResNet50, VGG16, or a transformer?

It reaches comparable accuracy with 5.3 million parameters instead of 25–138 million, which is what lets the whole service run on a free CPU container in 13 ms.

In detail: EfficientNet scales depth, width and resolution together rather than one at a time, so it sits on a better accuracy-per-parameter curve. A vision transformer would likely beat it given ImageNet-21k pretraining and a GPU — we have neither, and a model that cannot be served is not a product. The architecture is a swap of one line if that changes.

Q2. Your accuracy is 44%. Isn't that terrible?

Accuracy is the wrong measure here and we do not use it. The system makes one decision — refer or not — and on that it is right 81% of the time on referable cases.

In detail: 73% of the data is grade 0, so a model that always answered "No DR" would score 73% accuracy and be worthless. Screening is judged on sensitivity, specificity and quadratic weighted kappa. Ours: kappa 0.425, sensitivity 80.9%, specificity 60.3% on 7,026 held-out images — and 95.4% sensitivity on an Indian dataset it had never seen.

Q3. What is quadratic weighted kappa and why use it?

It measures agreement with human graders after subtracting the agreement you would get by chance, and it punishes big mistakes far more than small ones.

In detail: The grades are ordered, so calling a grade-4 eye "grade 0" is much worse than calling it "grade 3". Quadratic weighting penalises by the square of the distance. 1.0 is perfect, 0 is chance. Two human graders typically agree around 0.80; ours is 0.425 internally and 0.737 on IDRiD.

Q4. Why is your specificity so low? Won't you flood the clinic?

It is low on purpose. We chose the threshold to catch 4 in 5 referable cases, and that choice costs us 4 in 10 healthy people being referred.

In detail: Sensitivity and specificity trade against each other along one curve. Using the model's most likely grade gives 89% specificity but only 50% sensitivity — half the sick people sent home. For screening, a missed case costs sight; a false alarm costs an appointment. The threshold is a single number in a config file, so a programme that wants the other balance can move it — and the report prints whichever pair is in force.

Q5. How do you know the model isn't just memorising this dataset?

We ran it, unchanged, on two datasets from other countries and other cameras. Sensitivity held between 81% and 95% with no retraining.

In detail: On IDRiD (Indian, Kowa camera): 95.4% sensitivity, 65.3% specificity, kappa 0.737. On Messidor-2 (French, Topcon): 82.9% and 51.4%, kappa 0.549. The honest caveat from the same experiment: per-grade accuracy does not transfer — Moderate recall is 7% on IDRiD while Severe is 87%, so the model pushes middle grades outward and they land on the referable side. The referral decision generalises; the five-grade label does not.

Q6. Explain Grad-CAM. Is it real explainability or decoration?

It shows which parts of the retina moved the prediction, by weighting the final feature maps by how much each contributed to the chosen grade.

In detail: Our head is Global Average Pooling → Dense, and for that shape the Grad-CAM gradient weights are algebraically identical to the Dense weights — so the heatmap is a single forward pass, no autodiff, no TensorFlow at serving time. It is genuine attribution, not decoration, but it is coarse: it points at a region, not at a lesion. We present it as "where it looked", never as "this is the haemorrhage".

Q7. Why ONNX instead of just serving the Keras model?

The TensorFlow image was around 600 MB and would not fit the free tier; the ONNX model is 16 MB and answers in 13 ms.

In detail: ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) is a framework-independent format. We train in Keras, export once, and serve with ONNX Runtime. The export script refuses to write anything unless the two agree to within 1e-5 on random input; measured agreement is 8.6e-07.

Q8. Why microservices instead of one application?

The model changes on a different clock from the clinical workflow, and the AI service is the only part that needs heavy Python libraries.

In detail: Retraining and redeploying the model must not risk the auth, records or doctor review path. Splitting also lets the AI service scale on its own, and keeps a 600 MB ML dependency tree out of the service that holds patient data. The cost is network hops and three cold starts, which is the right trade here.

Q9. How is patient data protected?

JWT authentication, BCrypt-hashed passwords, role separation, and every image URL checks that the requester owns that record.

In detail: Tokens expire; passwords are never stored, only their BCrypt hash; a patient cannot open another patient's screening or a doctor-only route. Images are served through an ownership-checked endpoint rather than a public path. What is missing, honestly: encryption at rest is Atlas's default rather than something we configured, there is no rate limiting, and no audit log of who viewed what. Those are required before real patient data, and they are on the list.

Q10. What happens if the AI service goes down?

The health endpoint reports it, screening is blocked with an explanation instead of a failure, and everything already stored stays readable.

In detail: /api/health reports each of the three services independently. Grading is the only feature that hard-depends on the AI service; login, history, reports and doctor review continue. On the free tier a sleeping service takes ~50 s to wake, which the UI states plainly rather than hanging.

Q11. Can this run offline, in a village with no internet?

Not today. The architecture supports it — there is a docker-compose file — but we have not built or tested an offline deployment, so we do not claim it.

In detail: All three services plus MongoDB can run on one laptop on a local network; the model is 16 MB and the inference is CPU-only, so there is no technical obstacle. What is missing is a tested build, a sync path for when connectivity returns, and a way to update the model. Roughly half a day of work, not a redesign.

Q12. How long does one screening take?

13 ms of model time; about one second end to end locally, and 1–2 seconds through the free cloud tier.

In detail: Rejecting a non-eye takes 11–23 ms because the model never runs — roughly fifty times cheaper than grading, which is exactly why that check can run on every live frame. The rest is JPEG encoding, network and heatmap rendering.

Q13. Your training data is 73% healthy. How did you handle that?

Class weights during training, keeping every image of the rare grades, and judging the model on sensitivity rather than accuracy.

In detail: Weights are square-rooted inverse frequency — full inverse weighting makes one grade-4 image worth eight grade-0 images and turns the gradient into noise. The training set keeps all of grades 1–4 and caps only grade 0. An earlier version capped every class equally, which threw away 1,300 grade-2 images while doing nothing for grade 4.

Q14. What is the false negative rate, and what happens to those people?

About 1 in 5 referable cases is missed at our threshold. They are told to attend routine annual screening, so they are seen again — later than ideal, not never.

In detail: This is the number that matters most and we print it on every report. It is why the system is positioned as triage rather than diagnosis, why a doctor reviews every case, and why a low grade is never phrased as "you are fine". Moving the threshold to 90% sensitivity is one config change and costs specificity — a programme-level decision, not ours.

Q15. Why did you not use APTOS, the more famous dataset?

APTOS requires accepting Kaggle competition terms interactively, which cannot be automated. We used an MIT-licensed set of double the size instead.

In detail: 35,126 images against APTOS's 3,662. We did search for and verify ungated mirrors of APTOS, IDRiD, Messidor-2 and DDR, and used IDRiD and Messidor-2 for external validation — which is the more valuable role for a second dataset anyway.

Q16. How would you improve it with more time and a GPU?

An ordinal head, higher input resolution, and lesion-level labels — in that order.

In detail: (1) The head predicts five independent classes, but the grades are ordered and the decision is P(grade ≥ 2); four cumulative binary outputs would supervise that directly. (2) 224×224 makes one pixel roughly 50 µm while a microaneurysm is 15–60 µm — the earliest lesions are at the edge of what the input can carry; 512×512 needs a GPU. (3) IDRiD ships lesion masks, which would let the heatmap point at a haemorrhage instead of a region.

6. Domain and general-knowledge questions

Q17. What is diabetic retinopathy?

High blood sugar damages the tiny vessels at the back of the eye. They leak, close off, and the eye grows fragile new vessels that bleed.

In detail: It progresses through microaneurysms (grade 1), haemorrhages and exudates (grades 2–3), and finally new vessel growth — proliferative DR (grade 4) — which risks vitreous haemorrhage and retinal detachment. It is painless and symptomless until late, which is precisely why screening exists: by the time vision blurs, damage is already done.

Q18. How big is the problem in India?

Roughly 101 million people with diabetes (ICMR-INDIAB, 2023), around one in three of whom develop retinopathy — against roughly one ophthalmologist per 100,000 people.

In detail: Every diabetic is meant to have a dilated eye examination annually. The arithmetic does not work: the specialists do not exist in the numbers required, and they are concentrated in cities while most patients are not. DR is a leading cause of preventable blindness in working-age adults, and it is preventable precisely because early treatment works. The bottleneck is not knowledge — it is who gets looked at, in what order.

Q19. What is the treatment, and why does early detection matter so much?

Laser photocoagulation, anti-VEGF injections, or vitrectomy. Treatment prevents further loss but rarely restores vision already lost.

In detail: That asymmetry is the whole argument for screening. Catching grade 2 and treating it protects sight that still exists; catching grade 4 after a bleed means managing damage. This is also why a screening tool is tuned for sensitivity — the cost of the two error types is not symmetric.

Q20. Difference between screening and diagnosis?

Screening sorts a large healthy-looking population into "needs a closer look" and "does not". Diagnosis is the closer look, by a clinician.

In detail: A screening test is allowed to be imprecise in one direction: it should rarely miss disease, and may over-refer. That is the standard we designed to. RetiNova performs screening; a doctor performs the diagnosis, and every result carries that statement.

Q21. What is diabetic macular oedema, and why do you not detect it?

Fluid swelling at the macula, the part of the retina responsible for sharp central vision. We do not detect it because our labels do not contain it.

In detail: DME can blind someone at any retinopathy grade, so a "no DR" result does not rule it out — and our report says exactly that. Detecting it needs a dataset with macular oedema labels such as IDRiD's second task, or ideally OCT imaging, which a fundus camera cannot provide.

Q22. Why can a phone camera not photograph the retina?

The retina sits ~17 mm behind a 2–8 mm pupil. You must send light in and get the image back out through that same small opening without the cornea reflecting the light into the sensor.

In detail: Fundus cameras solve this with coaxial illumination and a condensing lens. A bare phone images the front of the eye and stops. That is why our retinal path needs a lens — from a ₹1,500 20-dioptre lens to a ₹15 lakh fundus camera — and why the camera-only path measures the person (pulse, pupil reflex, pallor) rather than the retina.

Q23. What do OD and OS mean?

Oculus dexter — right eye. Oculus sinister — left eye. (OU = both.)

In detail: Standard clinical notation, and recorded per screening. Laterality matters because the two eyes are graded and treated separately; a report that does not say which eye is clinically incomplete.

9. The three answers to have ready

"What is genuinely new here?" — Three things. It refuses images nobody could grade, before grading them. It works with no retinal hardware at all by measuring the person instead of the retina. And it decides referral at a stated operating point rather than by taking the model's favourite label — which is the difference between catching 65% and 81% of disease.

"What is the biggest weakness?" — Moderate (grade 2) recall is 20.5%, and that is exactly the referral boundary. The model separates obviously-healthy from obviously-diseased far better than it handles the middle. We know it, we measure it, and it is why a doctor reviews every case.

"Why should this win?" — Because it is measured rather than claimed. Every number in this document came from the running system, the failure modes are printed on the product itself, and it was validated on data from a different country and a different camera without being retrained to make the numbers look better.

RetiNova is a screening-support prototype, not a diagnostic device, and is not approved by any regulator. No output should be acted on without clinician review. Performance figures are measured on public research datasets and would require prospective validation on Indian screening populations before clinical deployment. Epidemiological figures are approximate and drawn from published sources (ICMR-INDIAB 2023 for diabetes prevalence).
